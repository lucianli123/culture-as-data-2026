# Project workshop kit

Group activities for the project weeks (5 to 8), when the plan says "project lab" and the
room would otherwise be twenty people working alone with headphones on.

Everything here runs in **trios**, takes a fixed number of minutes, and needs no preparation
from the instructor beyond a timer. Each activity ends with the maker writing one sentence
down, so there is something to carry into next week.

---

## The 90-minute workshop

Run this once, in Week 5 or Week 6, when everyone has data but nobody has a finding yet.
The order matters: people describe before they interpret, and defend only after they have
been understood.

| Time | Activity | Grouping |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00 | **Corpus tour** | trios, 4 min each |
| 0:15 | **Blind chart read** | trios, 6 min each |
| 0:35 | **Red team** | trios, 7 min each |
| 0:58 | Break | |
| 1:05 | **Scope surgery** | pairs, 8 min each |
| 1:22 | **Commit out loud** | whole room, 1 min each |

Two rules for the whole session. The maker does not defend during steps 1 to 3; they take
notes. And every activity names a **timekeeper** who is not the maker.

---

## 1 · Corpus tour (4 minutes each)

**Show the data, not the idea.** Screen-share the actual rows. Scroll. Say how many there
are, where they came from, and what one row is.

The listeners ask only:

- What counts as one row here?
- What is in this data that you did not choose to collect?
- What would be in it if you had collected it differently?

Nobody may say what the project is *about* until the four minutes are up.

**Why first:** most stuck projects are stuck because the question was written before anyone
looked at the rows. Saying "one row is one review, posted by one account, between 2019 and
2024" out loud fixes more than an hour of advice.

**Write down:** one sentence defining a row, and one thing your corpus cannot see.

---

## 2 · Blind chart read (6 minutes each)

The maker puts up **one chart** and says nothing at all.

- **2 min, silent.** Everyone looks. No talking.
- **2 min, describe.** Responders say only what they see: "the rust line is above the grey
  line in every panel," "there is a gap in the middle." No interpretation, no advice.
- **1 min, guess.** Each responder says what they think the chart is claiming.
- **1 min, maker.** The maker says what they meant it to claim.

**Why it works:** the gap between the guesses and the intent is the finding you have not
communicated yet. If all three responders guessed right, the chart is done. If they guessed
three different things, the problem is the chart, not the reader.

**Write down:** the wrongest guess, and one change that would have prevented it.

---

## 3 · Red team (7 minutes each)

The maker states one finding in one sentence. The trio then tries to break it, out loud,
with the maker silent for the first five minutes.

Work down this list in order. Stop when one of them lands.

1. **Something else got bigger.** Would this show up anyway from the corpus growing, the
   platform changing, or more of the thing being collected?
2. **The category is doing the work.** Who decided what counts? What happens to the finding
   if that line moves?
3. **A giveaway.** Is a model or a count keying on something that trivially marks the
   answer, like a name, a date stamp, or a boilerplate phrase?
4. **The sample.** Who is missing from this data entirely, and would they have changed it?
5. **It would look like this anyway.** If you shuffled the labels, how different would the
   picture be? (Week 2's shuffle test, applied by hand.)

Then the maker gets 2 minutes to answer the strongest objection.

**Why it works:** every one of these is an objection a reader will make silently and then
stop believing you. Hearing it from a friend in Week 6 is cheaper than from a stranger in
Week 10.

**Write down:** the objection you could not answer. That is your next week's work.

---

## 4 · Scope surgery (8 minutes each, in pairs)

The partner's job is to cut the project in half. Not to improve it.

- **3 min.** The maker describes everything they currently plan to do.
- **4 min.** The partner proposes the smallest version that would still be worth reading.
  They must name what gets cut, not hedge.
- **1 min.** The maker says which cut they accept.

**Why it works:** projects fail at the end for having four half-finished parts instead of
one finished one. A partner will cut what a maker cannot.

**Write down:** the one sentence your project is now about, and the two things you are no
longer doing.

---

## 5 · Commit out loud (1 minute each, whole room)

Standing, no screens. Each person says three things:

1. My question is ...
2. By next week I will have ...
3. The thing most likely to stop me is ...

The instructor writes point 3 down for everyone and reads the list back at the start of the
next session.

**Why last:** a commitment made to a room is kept more often than one made to a notebook,
and the list of blockers tells you what to teach next week.

## The rest of the bank

Swap any of these in, or use them to fill a lab slot in Weeks 6 to 8.

### Pre-mortem (15 min, trios)

It is Week 10. The project failed. Each person takes 3 minutes to write why, in past tense,
as specifically as they can. Then read them aloud and mark the ones that are already true.
Fix those.

### Null-result swap (15 min, pairs)

Trade a dead end. Your partner has 5 minutes to find the interesting question inside your
failure, and you inside theirs. "We looked and there was no relationship" is a reportable
result; "we gave up" is not. Pair this with the
[pivot kit](pivot-kit-corpora.md) if the corpus itself is the dead end.

### Method speed-dating (20 min, rotating pairs)

Four rounds, 5 minutes each, new partner every round. You get 90 seconds to state your
question; your partner spends the rest naming a method from the course that would answer
it, and one reason it might not. You keep every suggestion and choose afterwards.

### The stranger test (10 min, cross-group)

Hand your draft page to someone who has never heard about your project. They read it in
silence for 4 minutes, then say, without looking back at it: what the project found, and
what they would tell a friend about it. Anything they cannot say is not on the page yet.

### So-what ladder (10 min, trios)

State your finding. The trio asks "so what?" three times, and you have to answer each time.
Most findings die on the second rung, which is useful to know in Week 6 rather than Week 9.
Write down the third answer; it is usually the sentence your introduction needs.

### Caption clinic (10 min, trios)

Everyone brings one chart. Each person writes a one-sentence title for someone else's
chart, using only what they can see. The maker compares it to their own title. Whichever is
clearer wins, regardless of who wrote it.

## For the facilitator

Keep time out loud and visibly. Every activity here is short enough that running over is a
choice, not an accident.

Hold the no-defending rule in activities 1 to 3. Makers will start explaining within about
twenty seconds; interrupt kindly and every time, and the room learns it by the second
rotation.

Trios beat pairs. In a pair, one confident person sets the reading; in a trio, the third
person disagrees with the second and the maker gets two readings instead of one.

Do not join a trio as a fourth. Float, listen from outside, and collect what you hear for
the whole-room close. Your presence turns a workshop into a tutorial.

This kit is separate from the Week 9
[Critical Response Process](critical-response-process.md) on purpose. That protocol is for
finished drafts, and it is built to protect the maker. These activities are for work in
progress, and their job is to find the problems while there is still time to fix them.
